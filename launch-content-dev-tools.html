Matching your content to every product and feature launch

How we did it at DeepInfra.

Read the guide See the case studies

Most feature launches get a lot of hype inside the company, but struggle to reach their intended audience.

We think product and feature launches can also be used as marketing tools to attract new customers. Being calculated and loud about your features and releases can help you show up in feeds, in Google and influence LLM searches.

This guide goes into the methodology we use to identify opportunities and execute content around feature releases. The working example is our work with deepinfra.com. They are in an extremely competitive industry, since their product releases are based around open-source AI models, and every time they get a model so do all of their competitors. We need to be efficient, quick and produce content that is high quality to win in search and get eyeballs.

TL;DR

  • Decide which releases deserve what before anyone writes a word. Three kinds of release, three kits.
  • The page comes before the post. If the feature does not have a page, search and AI assistants do not know you have it.
  • Pick the prompts the launch has to win, and build a page for each one.
  • Write the announcement from a brief, not from the pull request. A 25-minute interview and six fields.
  • Template the releases that repeat, so the fifth integration launch costs an hour instead of a week.
  • Spend the two weeks after launch on mentions. Communities and distribution platforms for the fast ones, outreach for the slow ones. That is what AI assistants cite, and it is the step most teams skip.
  • Score every launch on one sheet. It is the only way to know which kind of release deserves a full launch next time.
Step 1

Decide what kind of release this is

The first thing we decide on every release is which kind it is, and we decide it before anyone opens a doc. We use three kinds, with a test for each:

ReleaseThe testWhat goes out
Full launchIt changes who the product is for, or what a developer can do with it in a dayThe full kit: a feature page, an announcement, docs and changelog, a demo, posts where developers gather, an email to users (Steps 2 to 7)
Changelog plus postExisting users would want to know; new users would not switch for itChangelog entry, docs updated, a short post on X and in Discord
Changelog onlyA fix, a flag, a version bump nobody will search forChangelog entry and docs. Nothing else, on purpose.

Then we write the release calendar from the engineering roadmap, a quarter out, with the kind marked on each row. Marketing follows engineering dates here, not the other way round, and the calendar is what lets us prepare pages (Step 2) before the feature exists rather than after the announcement has gone out.

We add one more test in some categories: will a competitor ship the same thing this week? If yes, the release is a race, and the deadline collapses from "when it is ready" to "the same day as the announcement." I cover that case in Step 9, because it is where our strongest evidence comes from. For most launches, most of the time, the three rows above are enough.

Pro tip: in our experience founders over-count full launches and engineers under-count them. We break the tie with the test in the first row. If a developer could not do something new in a day because of it, it is not a full launch, however long it took to build.

Step 2

Prepare your content before your public release

If a page does not exist, search and AI assistants assume the feature does not. Launch posts are time-stamped and sink; a feature page on a stable URL is what gets found and cited six months later. So we build the page first, and the announcement points at it, not the other way round.

What the feature page carries

In the order a developer reads it. Developers skip the marketing paragraph and read the table and the code; so does the assistant about to summarize you. We write those first.

ElementThe rule
The feature's nameIn the title, URL and first sentence, spelled the way people will search for it
What it doesOne sentence, from the positioning page
The specificsLimits, pricing, supported languages or frameworks, the endpoint; in a table
A working snippetTested by someone who did not build the feature
What changedAgainst the previous version, for the migration intent
One limitationStated plainly; it is what makes the rest credible
The next stepSomething a developer can do in five minutes: run it, the playground, free credits

Which supporting pages, and when

We do not build all five for every launch. We pick the ones that match the questions developers and the assistants are asking (Step 3 is how we find out).

PageBuild it whenQuery it catches
Pricing or plan pageThe feature changes what things cost"[feature] pricing"
Comparison or "best of" pageDevelopers will shortlist you against alternatives for this; the format AI assistants cite"[product] vs [competitor] [feature]", "best [category] with [feature]"
Integration guideThere is code to copy"[feature] with [language or framework]"

We do the keyword research a week out, from the queries in the right-hand column with the product's names filled in.

Step 3

AI visibility: pick the prompts this launch has to win

Keyword research tells us what people type into Google. It tells us nothing about what they ask ChatGPT, Perplexity or Gemini, and for a developer-tools launch that is now the question that matters. When a developer asks an assistant whether your product does X, or where they should run a model, the answer is a yes, a no, or a competitor's name, and the launch content decides which. So before we build the pages in Step 2, we decide which prompts this release has to win and build the pages against that list.

Three types of prompt, and a page for each

We recommend three types of prompt per launch, and we create a new page for each core prompt rather than hoping the announcement covers it. The announcement never does; it is dated and it sinks.

Prompt typeWhat a developer asksThe page we build to answer it
The yes/no"Does [product] support X?" "Can I run [model] on [product]?"The feature or model page (Step 2), with the feature's name spelled the way the prompt spells it, in the title and first sentence
The shortlist"Best [category] tool with X." "Where should I run [model]?" "Cheapest [model] API."A comparison or "best of" page with your product as one honest entry next to the competitors. This is the prompt that produces recommendations, and the page type assistants cite most.
The how-to"How do I do X with [product]?" "[Model] with Python."The integration guide, with the snippet the assistant can quote

We weight the shortlist prompts highest. A yes/no prompt gets you confirmed; a shortlist prompt gets you recommended, and that is where a launch turns into a new customer.

How we find the prompts

  • Start with the people who asked for the feature. The sales calls and tickets that requested it are phrased the way buyers phrase it, and the assistants get asked in the same words. "Can it read a passport MRZ" is a prompt; "document data extraction capability" is not.
  • Reuse the shape from the last three launches. The prompts for a new model look like the prompts for the previous model with the name changed. We keep the list and fill in the blanks.
  • Ask the assistants what they get asked. Put the feature name into ChatGPT and Perplexity and read the follow-up suggestions; they are a cheap view of the real question set.
  • Keep it to five to ten. Fifty prompts per release is a dashboard nobody reads. Five to ten, weighted toward shortlists, is a list someone acts on.
On a real account: DeepInfra

We run about 50 prompts for DeepInfra across the inference category ("fastest Kimi K3 API provider," "cheapest way to run DeepSeek V4," "best Llama hosting"). The week a model drops, the five or six prompts for that model are the only numbers anyone looks at, and the answer usually flips within two weeks of the main page and the "best providers" page going live. By the time we are tracking the next model, deepinfra.com is in the previous one's answer. Case study

Step 4

Write the announcement from a brief, not from the pull request

Launch posts drift furthest from the positioning, because they get written last, by the person who built the feature, from the PR description. Our fix is a six-field brief, filled from a 25-minute interview with that engineer, and an announcement we write from the brief in the positioning language. The engineer does not write; they answer questions and check the code.

FieldWhere it comes from
What is new, in one sentence a developer would repeatThe engineer, then rewritten against the positioning page
Who it is for, and the moment they need itThe sales calls and tickets that asked for it; the positioning page's audience line
What they can do now that they could not yesterdayThe engineer: "what does this let someone build?"
How to try it in five minutesThe snippet, the flag, the playground link; tested by someone who did not build it
One numberA benchmark, a limit, a latency, a price. The engineer: "what would you put on a slide?"
One limitation, stated plainlyThe engineer: "what do you wish was documented?" The caveat is what makes the rest credible.

The announcement is those six fields in that order, with the demo embedded. We record the demo once, with whoever can do it in one take, and cut it to 60 seconds for X. Nothing else about the post is clever. "What you can do now" beats "what we shipped" every time, and the limitation paragraph is the one developers quote back approvingly.

Pro tip: we have someone who did not build the feature run the "how to try it" step before publishing. If it takes them more than five minutes, the post says "in five minutes" and is wrong, and the first comment will say so.

Step 5

Build a template for the releases that repeat

Some launches are one-offs. Most are not: the tenth integration, the fourth SDK, the new model of the week. For anything we will launch more than four times, we hand-write the first four and extract a template from them; from the fifth on, the brief plus the template produce the draft. The engineer fills the fields, the template holds the structure and the voice, and one of us reads the output before it goes live.

Launch contentTemplatable?Why
Integration pages, model pages, SDK pagesYesSame shape every time: what it is, the specifics, the snippet, the limit, the next step
Supporting pricing, comparison and "what changed" pagesYesThe structure never changes; the facts do, and the facts are in the brief
Changelog-plus-post releasesYesSix fields, 150 words, the same every week
The announcement for a full launchNoIt is the one piece that has to sound like a person who cares. We write it by hand, from the brief.

We build the template in a tool we control. We use AirOps for templated volume and Claude for one-off drafts; whichever AI builder your team already has works, as long as the brief and the fields go in and a draft comes out with every step visible. Then our editing pass, which is not optional: we strip the model's meta commentary ("in this article we will…"), verify every number against the brief, three rounds, because the model will round, convert or helpfully update a figure it was not given, rewrite the passive voice, and break any sentence over 40 words. It takes us under an hour for a set of pages, and skipping it is how a wrong price ends up on the page developers compare you on.

On a real account: DeepInfra

DeepInfra adds a new model most weeks, so every release fills the same slots from one brief through an AirOps template we built once: the main model page, a pricing guide, a "best API providers for [model]" comparison with DeepInfra as one honest entry, an integration guide, a benchmarks piece and a version overview. The engineer supplies the price, context window, throughput and snippet; the editing pass takes an hour; the whole set is live the same day. Step 6

The launch week, with an owner per row

We run a launch as a checklist that starts a week before the release and ends two weeks after it. We wrote it once, put a name on every row, and run it on every full launch without re-deciding anything.

The row most teams skip is the internal linking, and in my experience it is the highest-leverage ten minutes in the table. A new page nobody links to is invisible to the crawler and to the assistants; five links from pages Google already visits daily is the difference between indexed this afternoon and indexed next week.

Step 7

Where it goes, under whose name

The announcement is one URL waiting to be found until it is posted where developers are. We put the same launch out in five forms on the same day, because that is how it gets found, discussed and cited, and each form feeds the others: the Reddit thread links the page, the X thread sends the founder's followers to both, and an assistant that sees the feature described in five places treats it as established.

ChannelFormWho postsWhen
Your siteThe announcement, linking to the feature pageThe writer, under their nameFirst, so there is a URL to cite
XA short thread: what you can do now, the demo, the snippet, the link lastThe founder or the engineer; developers follow people, not brandsWithin the hour
RedditNot the announcement. A build guide where the feature is one tool in a working stack, or a complete answer in the thread where someone asked for exactly thisDevRel, under a real nameDay 1 to 3
Discord or Slack communityThe announcement, plus "ask me anything about it this week"The engineerLaunch day
Hacker NewsOnly for full launches that would interest people who have never heard of you, submitted plainly, with the engineer in the commentsThe founderLaunch day, morning US time
Email to usersWhat changed, how to try it, two paragraphsWhoever owns the productLaunch day
LinkedInOne takeaway in the author's voice, the demo inline, link in the first commentPersonal accounts, never the company page aloneLaunch day

Our rule across all of them: real names. Developers trust the engineer who built it and the founder who bet on it, and they can tell a company account from a person in one line. On Reddit it is more than a trust issue; persona accounts posting announcements is how we lost accounts early on, and nothing earns a ban faster. The build guide, with the feature as one tool among several and the alternatives named, is what we have seen survive and get upvoted.

Step 8

After launch day: links, mentions, and keeping it alive

Mentions beat links. When a developer asks an assistant whether your product does X, the answer is assembled from the comparison lists, roundups and threads the assistant trusts; your own feature page is the least of it. In our tracking, a plain-text mention in a "best tools for X" article that already ranks is worth more than a backlink from a site nobody reads.

The honest caveat: earning those mentions is slow. Getting added to a roundup that already ranks means finding the author, writing the ask, and waiting weeks for an editor who may never reply, and a launch does not have weeks. So we run two tracks in parallel: the slow outreach that pays off over a quarter, and the fast channels that put the launch in front of developers and in the assistants' source set within days.

The fast track: communities and distribution platforms

We recommend working with communities like Reddit and distribution platforms like Medium, dev.to and Substack to get reach and quick mentions in the first week. Each one is a place developers already read, each one is crawled and cited by the assistants, and none of them needs anyone's permission.

ChannelWhat goes thereWhy it works fast
RedditA complete answer in the threads where someone asked for exactly this, and a build guide where the feature is one tool in a working stack, under a real nameThreads rank for years and are a primary source for AI Overviews; a top comment shows up in Google's result snippet
dev.toThe integration guide or build guide, republished with a canonical link back to your pageIndexed within hours, high domain authority, a developer audience that reads tutorials
MediumThe benchmark or opinion piece, in a relevant publication if you can get into oneReach beyond your own list; assistants cite Medium for comparisons and explainers
Substack or your newsletterThe launch note to people who already know you, with the demoExisting users are the first to try it, and the first to mention it elsewhere

Republishing is the point, not a shortcut. The same integration guide on your site, on dev.to with a canonical, and summarized in a Reddit answer is three mentions of the feature in three places the assistants read, in one week, from one piece of writing. We set the canonical tag so the search credit stays with your page.

The slow track: the lists that already rank

  • We reach out to the roundups and comparison articles that already rank for the category, asking to be added as one entry with the real price and the real limitation. We keep the list from launch to launch; the same ten articles rank every time, and the editors who added you once will add you again.
  • We update your own comparison and "best of" pages the week it ships. A "best X providers" page on your own site that does not mention this week's feature is telling the assistant you do not have it.
  • Consistency across surfaces. We check that the feature's name, price and limit read the same on the page, in the docs, in the changelog, in the README, in the dev.to copy and in the Reddit answer. Assistants gain confidence from agreement across sources and lose it from a stale price in one of them.
  • A quarterly "what changed" round-up, one line per release, linking to each feature page. It is the page that catches "what does [product] support," and it is the cheapest page we build.

We track the launch prompts from Step 3 in PromptWatch until the product is in the answer, and after, because the next release moves the answer again.

Step 9

When it's a race: how DeepInfra launches a model a week

Everything above assumes you set the pace. Some categories do not let you. DeepInfra hosts 100+ models and adds a new one most weeks, and so do Together AI, Fireworks, Groq, Replicate and Novita, usually within hours of the same model lab's announcement. The feature is a commodity before the announcement thread has cooled. What separates the providers is which one developers find, and which one the assistants name when someone asks where to run it. That is a race, and we run it on content.

When we started, a new DeepInfra model page took days to get indexed. By the time Google saw it, the comparison lists and the Reddit threads had formed around Together AI and Replicate, and the AI answers followed them. The content was fine; the pipeline was slow, and in a race slow loses regardless of what you wrote. What we changed is the same process as the rest of this guide, compressed to a day:

  • We do everything that can be done before the model exists, before. We rebuilt the crawl infrastructure so a page published at 10am is indexed by the afternoon; we keep templates with the fields blank; we pick keywords the moment a model is rumoured, from the pattern of the last ten releases; the outreach list is ready.
  • On launch day we fill fields. The main model page and the supporting bundle (Step 5) go live the same day as the announcement, from one brief, with internal links from the five highest-traffic pages.
  • Then two weeks of mentions. We answer the "where can I run [model]" threads in r/LocalLLaMA, r/LLMDevs, r/DeepSeek and r/openrouter completely, every provider named, within hours. We reach out to the "best providers for [model]" roundups that already rank. We post the benchmarks piece where the model is being discussed.

It is the strongest evidence we have for the whole process, because the pace leaves nowhere to hide. If your category works this way, the deadline for every row in Step 6 moves to the same day, and the preparation in Steps 2 and 3 becomes the job.

Step 10

Score every launch the same way

One sheet, one row per release, the same columns every time. We score a launch on three things, in the order they happen: did search see it, did people visit it, and did the assistants pick it up. Then whether any of those visits turned into a conversion. The point is not the numbers on any one launch; it is seeing launches side by side, which is what tells you which kind of release deserves a full launch next time and which part of the kit is slipping.

Three sources, one row

SourceWhat we pullWhat it tells you
Google Search ConsoleImpressions and clicks for the feature page and its supporting pages, and for the queries containing the feature's name, at day 7 and day 30Whether search saw the pages at all (impressions), whether anyone is looking for this (query impressions), and whether they chose you (clicks). Impressions with no clicks is a title problem; no impressions is an indexing problem.
GA4Sessions landing on the launch pages, by source: organic, direct, Reddit, dev.to, Medium, the newsletter, the assistants' referralsWhere the visitors came from, which is the only way to know whether the fast-track channels (Step 8) or search did the work. A launch that got 2,000 sessions from the newsletter and 40 from organic is a different lesson from the reverse.
PromptWatchPresence on the launch prompts from Step 3, and citations for the feature page, at day 14 and day 30Whether the assistants know you have it, and whether they credit your page or someone's roundup for it

Then the conversions

Visits are the input; the row is not finished until it says what the visits did. We tie every launch page to the conversion events from the companion guide (signup, API key, SDK install or download, with landing page and source), and add one column for the launch itself: first use of the new feature by a new account, which is the API call with the new model string or the flag turned on. The ratio that matters is conversions per 100 sessions on the launch pages. It is usually higher than the site average, because a developer who searched for the feature by name arrived ready to try it, and when it is lower than the site average the page is missing its next step.

The sheet also settles arguments for us. When a launch underperforms, the row shows whether search never saw the page, visitors came and did not convert, or the assistants never picked it up. Those have different fixes, and without the row I have watched teams pick the fix that is most fun.

Step 11

Who owns what, and when to hire

A launch process survives on names, not goodwill. We give every release one named owner, and split the rest so that nobody's part takes more than an hour or two once the templates exist.

The first full launch takes three times that, because the templates and the outreach list do not exist yet. By the fourth it is routine. When it stops being routine, that is the signal we tell clients to hire on: releases coming faster than one owner can run them alongside their actual job, rows in the scorecard going empty, or the sheet showing that launch pages are now a main source of conversions and the mention work is what moves the AI column, so it deserves more than the hour it gets.

The first marketing hire then inherits something we rarely see a first hire get: a release calendar with the kind marked on every row, a template set, an outreach list with live contacts, a Reddit presence under real names, and a scorecard with a row for every launch. Their first quarter is adding to it, not building it.
