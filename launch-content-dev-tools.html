Matching your content to every product and feature launch

How we did it at DeepInfra.

Read the guide See the case studies

Most feature launches get a lot of hype inside the company, but struggle to reach their intended audience.

We think product and feature launches can also be used as marketing tools to attract new customers. Being calculated and loud about your features and releases can help you show up in feeds, in Google and influence LLM searches.

This guide goes into the methodology we use to identify opportunities and execute content around feature releases. The working example is our work with deepinfra.com. They are in an extremely competitive industry, since their product releases are based around open-source AI models, and every time they get a model so do all of their competitors. We need to be efficient, quick and produce content that is high quality to win in search and get eyeballs.

TL;DR

  • Decide which releases deserve what before anyone writes a word. Three kinds of release, three kits.
  • The page comes before the post. If the feature does not have a page, search and AI assistants do not know you have it.
  • Write the announcement from a brief, not from the pull request. A 25-minute interview and six fields.
  • Template the releases that repeat, so the fifth integration launch costs an hour instead of a week.
  • Spend the two weeks after launch on links and mentions. That is what AI assistants cite, and it is the step most teams skip.
  • Score every launch on one sheet. It is the only way to know which kind of release deserves a full launch next time.
Step 1

Decide what kind of release this is

The first decision on every release is which kind it is, and it gets made before anyone opens a doc. Three kinds, with a test for each:

ReleaseThe testWhat goes out
Full launchIt changes who the product is for, or what a developer can do with it in a dayThe full kit: a feature page, an announcement, docs and changelog, a demo, posts where developers gather, an email to users (Steps 2 to 6)
Changelog plus postExisting users would want to know; new users would not switch for itChangelog entry, docs updated, a short post on X and in Discord
Changelog onlyA fix, a flag, a version bump nobody will search forChangelog entry and docs. Nothing else, on purpose.

Then write the release calendar from the engineering roadmap, a quarter out, with the kind marked on each row. Marketing follows engineering dates here, not the other way round, and the calendar is what lets you prepare pages (Step 2) before the feature exists rather than after the announcement has gone out.

One more test, which only applies in some categories: will a competitor ship the same thing this week? If yes, the release is a race, and the deadline collapses from "when it is ready" to "the same day as the announcement." Step 8 covers that case, because it is where our strongest evidence comes from. For most launches, most of the time, the three rows above are enough.

Pro tip: founders over-count full launches and engineers under-count them. The tie-breaker is the test in the first row. If a developer could not do something new in a day because of it, it is not a full launch, however long it took to build.

Step 2

The pages that have to exist before launch day

If a page does not exist, search and AI assistants assume the feature does not. Launch posts are time-stamped and sink; a feature page on a stable URL is what gets found and cited six months later. So the page is built first, and the announcement points at it, not the other way round.

What the feature page must carry, in the order a developer reads it: the feature's name in the title, URL and first sentence, spelled the way people will search for it; what it does, in one sentence from the positioning page; the specifics (limits, pricing, supported languages or frameworks, the endpoint); a working snippet; what is different from before; one limitation stated plainly; and a next step a developer can take in five minutes. Developers skip the marketing paragraph and read the table and the code. So does the assistant about to summarize you. Write those first.

The supporting pages depend on the feature, and you do not need all five for every launch. A pricing or plan page when the feature changes what things cost. A comparison or "best of" page when developers will shortlist you against alternatives for this, because that is the format AI assistants cite. An integration guide when there is code to copy. A benchmark when you have a number nobody else has. A "what changed" page when existing users have to migrate. Pick the ones that match the questions, and do the keyword research a week out: "[feature] [product]", "[feature] pricing", "[product] vs [competitor] [feature]", "[competitor] alternative with [feature]".

Step 3

Write the announcement from a brief, not from the pull request

Launch posts drift furthest from the positioning, because they get written last, by the person who built the feature, from the PR description. The fix is a six-field brief, filled from a 25-minute interview with that engineer, and an announcement written from the brief in the positioning language. The engineer does not write; they answer questions and check the code.

FieldWhere it comes from
What is new, in one sentence a developer would repeatThe engineer, then rewritten against the positioning page
Who it is for, and the moment they need itThe sales calls and tickets that asked for it; the positioning page's audience line
What they can do now that they could not yesterdayThe engineer: "what does this let someone build?"
How to try it in five minutesThe snippet, the flag, the playground link; tested by someone who did not build it
One numberA benchmark, a limit, a latency, a price. The engineer: "what would you put on a slide?"
One limitation, stated plainlyThe engineer: "what do you wish was documented?" The caveat is what makes the rest credible.

The announcement is those six fields in that order, with the demo embedded. The demo gets recorded once, by whoever can do it in one take, and cut to 60 seconds for X. Nothing else about the post is clever. "What you can do now" beats "what we shipped" every time, and the limitation paragraph is the one developers quote back approvingly.

Pro tip: have the person who did not build the feature run the "how to try it" step before publishing. If it takes them more than five minutes, the post says "in five minutes" and is wrong, and the first comment will say so.

Step 4

Build a template for the releases that repeat

Some launches are one-offs. Most are not: the tenth integration, the fourth SDK, the new model of the week. For anything you will launch more than four times, the first four are hand-written and become the exemplars a template is extracted from; from the fifth on, the brief plus the template produce the draft. The engineer fills the fields, the template holds the structure and the voice, and a person reads the output before it goes live.

Launch contentTemplatable?Why
Integration pages, model pages, SDK pagesYesSame shape every time: what it is, the specifics, the snippet, the limit, the next step
Supporting pricing, comparison and "what changed" pagesYesThe structure never changes; the facts do, and the facts are in the brief
Changelog-plus-post releasesYesSix fields, 150 words, the same every week
The announcement for a full launchNoIt is the one piece that has to sound like a person who cares. Write it by hand, from the brief.

Build the template in a tool you control. We use AirOps for templated volume and Claude for one-off drafts; whichever AI builder your team already has works, as long as the brief and the fields go in and a draft comes out with every step visible. Then the editing pass, which is not optional: strip the model's meta commentary ("in this article we will…"), verify every number against the brief, three rounds, because the model will round, convert or helpfully update a figure it was not given, rewrite the passive voice, and break any sentence over 40 words. It takes under an hour for a set of pages, and skipping it is how a wrong price ends up on the page developers compare you on.

On a real account: DeepInfra

DeepInfra adds a new model most weeks, so every release fills the same slots from one brief through an AirOps template we built once: the main model page, a pricing guide, a "best API providers for [model]" comparison with DeepInfra as one honest entry, an integration guide, a benchmarks piece and a version overview. The engineer supplies the price, context window, throughput and snippet; the editing pass takes an hour; the whole set is live the same day. Step 5

The launch week, with an owner per row

A launch is a checklist that starts a week before the release and ends two weeks after it. Write it once, put a name on every row, and run it on every full launch without re-deciding anything.

WhenWhatWho
A week beforeKeyword research for the feature's name and the questions around it. The feature page and supporting pages drafted from the template (Step 4). The brief filled from the engineer interview (Step 3).Whoever writes most; the engineer for 25 minutes
Two days beforeAnnouncement written from the brief and checked against the positioning page. Demo recorded. The "try it in five minutes" step tested by someone who did not build it.The writer; the checker; a volunteer
The day beforeDocs and changelog live. Feature page live on its stable URL, submitted in Search Console. User email queued.DevRel or PM
Launch dayAnnouncement published, linking to the feature page. Internal links to the feature page from the five highest-traffic pages on the site. Posts where developers gather, within the hour, under real names (Step 6). Stay in the threads and answer.The launch owner; founder; DevRel

The row most teams skip is the internal linking, and it is the highest-leverage ten minutes in the table. A new page nobody links to is invisible to the crawler and to the assistants; five links from pages Google already visits daily is the difference between indexed this afternoon and indexed next week.

Step 6

Where it goes, under whose name

The announcement is one URL waiting to be found until it is posted where developers are. The same launch, in five forms, on the same day, gets found, discussed and cited, and each form feeds the others: the Reddit thread links the page, the X thread sends the founder's followers to both, and an assistant that sees the feature described in five places treats it as established.

ChannelFormWho postsWhen
Your siteThe announcement, linking to the feature pageThe writer, under their nameFirst, so there is a URL to cite
XA short thread: what you can do now, the demo, the snippet, the link lastThe founder or the engineer; developers follow people, not brandsWithin the hour
RedditNot the announcement. A build guide where the feature is one tool in a working stack, or a complete answer in the thread where someone asked for exactly thisDevRel, under a real nameDay 1 to 3
Discord or Slack communityThe announcement, plus "ask me anything about it this week"The engineerLaunch day
Hacker NewsOnly for full launches that would interest people who have never heard of you, submitted plainly, with the engineer in the commentsThe founderLaunch day, morning US time
Email to usersWhat changed, how to try it, two paragraphsWhoever owns the productLaunch day
LinkedInOne takeaway in the author's voice, the demo inline, link in the first commentPersonal accounts, never the company page aloneLaunch day

The rule across all of them: real names. Developers trust the engineer who built it and the founder who bet on it, and they can tell a company account from a person in one line. On Reddit it is more than a trust issue; persona accounts posting announcements is how we lost accounts early on, and nothing earns a ban faster. The build guide, with the feature as one tool among several and the alternatives named, is what survives and gets upvoted.

Step 7

After launch day: links, mentions, and keeping it alive

Most launch content dies in 72 hours because nobody touches it after launch day. The feature is still being searched for six months later, and the AI assistants are still forming an opinion about whether you have it. Two weeks of follow-through is what decides that opinion, and it is the step most teams skip because the launch feels over.

Mentions beat links. When a developer asks an assistant whether your product does X, the answer is assembled from the comparison lists, roundups and threads the assistant trusts; your own feature page is the least of it. A plain-text mention in a "best tools for X" article that already ranks is worth more than a backlink from a site nobody reads. So the second week is spent getting named:

  • Outreach to the roundups and comparison articles that already rank for the category, asking to be added as one entry with the real price and the real limitation. Keep the list from launch to launch; the same ten articles rank every time.
  • The threads where people asked for this. Someone on Reddit, Stack Overflow or in a GitHub issue asked for exactly this feature in the last year. Find them, answer completely, link the page. Then go for the top comment on the threads that already rank for the feature's name; Google pulls top comments into the result snippet.
  • Your own comparison and "best of" pages updated the week it ships. A "best X providers" page on your own site that does not mention this week's feature is telling the assistant you do not have it.
  • Consistency across surfaces. The feature's name, price and limit should read the same on the page, in the docs, in the changelog, in the README and in the Reddit answer. Assistants gain confidence from agreement across sources and lose it from a stale price in one of them.
  • A quarterly "what changed" round-up, one line per release, linking to each feature page. It is the page that catches "what does [product] support," and it is cheap.

Track the prompts that matter ("does [product] support X," "best [category] tool with X") in PromptWatch until you are in the answer, and keep tracking after, because the next release moves the answer again.

Step 8

When it's a race: how DeepInfra launches a model a week

Everything above assumes you set the pace. Some categories do not let you. DeepInfra hosts 100+ models and adds a new one most weeks, and so do Together AI, Fireworks, Groq, Replicate and Novita, usually within hours of the same model lab's announcement. The feature is a commodity before the announcement thread has cooled. What separates the providers is which one developers find, and which one the assistants name when someone asks where to run it. That is a race, and it is run on content.

When we started, a new DeepInfra model page took days to get indexed. By the time Google saw it, the comparison lists and the Reddit threads had formed around Together AI and Replicate, and the AI answers followed them. The content was fine; the pipeline was slow, and in a race slow loses regardless of what you wrote. What changed is the same process as the rest of this guide, compressed to a day:

  • Everything that can be done before the model exists is done before. Crawl infrastructure rebuilt so a page published at 10am is indexed by the afternoon; templates with the fields blank; keywords picked the moment a model is rumoured, from the pattern of the last ten releases; the outreach list ready.
  • Launch day is filling fields. The main model page and the supporting bundle (Step 4) go live the same day as the announcement, from one brief, with internal links from the five highest-traffic pages.
  • Then two weeks of mentions. The "where can I run [model]" threads in r/LocalLLaMA, r/LLMDevs, r/DeepSeek and r/openrouter answered completely, every provider named, within hours. Outreach to the "best providers for [model]" roundups that already rank. The benchmarks piece posted where the model is being discussed.

It is the strongest evidence we have for the whole process, because the pace leaves nowhere to hide. If your category works this way, the deadline for every row in Step 5 moves to the same day, and the preparation in Step 2 becomes the job.

On a real account: DeepInfra

With the pipeline, the bundle and the mentions running on every release: blog clicks from Google went from 1,347 to 18,551 year over year, signups from organic sessions landing on the blog from 9 to 464, and organic sign-ups per session from 2.9% to 8.6%. In Gemini, deepinfra.com is now the most-cited domain for the inference prompts we track, with 563 citations in four weeks against 382 for artificialanalysis.ai and 353 for OpenRouter; the single most-cited page is a model-release analysis with 112 citations. Step 9

Score every launch the same way

One sheet, one row per release, the same columns every time. The point is not the numbers on any one launch; it is seeing launches side by side, which is what tells you which kind of release deserves a full launch next time and which part of the kit is slipping.

ColumnWhenWhat it tells you
Hours from publish to feature page indexedDay 1Whether the page and the internal links did their job
Impressions and clicks on the feature's nameDay 7 and day 30Whether anyone is looking for it, and whether they found you
Mentions earned: roundups, lists, threadsDay 14Whether the follow-through happened
AI citations for the feature page, and presence on the "does [product] support X" promptDay 30Whether the assistants know you have it
Conversions attributed to the launch pagesDay 30Whether any of it mattered
First use of the feature by a new accountDay 30Whether the developers who signed up used the thing you launched

The sheet also settles arguments. When a launch underperforms, the row shows whether the page was late, the mentions never landed, or the feature just was not interesting to anyone outside the building. Those have different fixes, and without the row the team picks the fix that is most fun.

Step 10

Who owns what, and when to hire

A launch process survives on names, not goodwill. Every release gets one named owner, and the rest is split so that nobody's part takes more than an hour or two once the templates exist.

WhoOwnsTime per full launch
Launch owner (rotates; never nobody)The calendar, the kind-of-release call, the checklist, the scorecard row2 hours
EngineerThe 25-minute interview; a 20-minute review of the page's numbers and code, not its wording; the Discord AMA1 hour
Whoever writes most (DevRel, or your author)The brief, the announcement, the feature page and supporting pages from the template, the editing pass, the internal linksHalf a day
DevRelThe demo; the Reddit build guide and thread answers, under a real name3 hours over two weeks
Outreach owner (the founder, until there is marketing)The roundup asks, from the standing list1 hour
FounderThe X thread; Hacker News when it qualifies30 minutes
The checkerReads the announcement and the feature page against the positioning page before anything goes live30 minutes

The first full launch takes three times that, because the templates and the outreach list do not exist yet. By the fourth it is routine. When it stops being routine, that is the signal to hire: releases coming faster than one owner can run them alongside their actual job, rows in the scorecard going empty, or the sheet showing that launch pages are now a main source of conversions and the mention work is what moves the AI column, so it deserves more than the hour it gets.

The first marketing hire then inherits something few first hires get: a release calendar with the kind marked on every row, a template set, an outreach list with live contacts, a Reddit presence under real names, and a scorecard with a row for every launch. Their first quarter is adding to it, not building it.
